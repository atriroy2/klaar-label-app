Kavita: Collab engine architecture. I want to understand your thinking before you go too deep.

Rohan: Yeah, so the vision is a real-time collaborative editing system, like Google Docs for project management. Multiple users editing simultaneously with conflict-free synchronization.

Kavita: Right. Timeline is July, OKR is to ship v1.

Rohan: Exactly. The way I'm thinking about it is CRDT-based. Conflict-free replicated data type. It gives us beautiful eventual consistency without the complexity of operational transforms.

Kavita: CRDTs are mathematically clean, but they're also complex to implement. Have you looked at OT as a starting point?

Rohan: OT feels like a crutch. It's older, less proven. If we're going to build this, let's do it right the first time.

Kavita: "Right" meaning mathematically pure?

Rohan: Means conflict-free without a central authority. OT requires a central server to resolve conflicts. That's a bottleneck.

Kavita: Is a bottleneck the problem we're trying to solve? Or is it a feature? Knowing which writes came first is useful.

Rohan: In a CRDT, the causal relationship is preserved anyway. Causality is built in.

Kavita: I hear you. But let me ask — how long does it take to build a CRDT system versus an OT system?

Rohan: [pause] CRDTs are new. Maybe eight to ten weeks if we're moving fast.

Kavita: Versus OT?

Rohan: Four, maybe five weeks.

Kavita: So we're talking double the complexity and double the time. For what benefit in v1?

Rohan: We don't want to build tech debt.

Kavita: I'm not suggesting we build something broken. I'm suggesting we build something that works, that scales, that our customers like. And we can improve it in v2 if we need to.

Rohan: Building OT now means rewriting it later.

Kavita: Maybe. Or maybe it works well enough and we don't touch it. Most systems don't need mathematical purity.

Rohan: This one's different. It's core infrastructure.

Kavita: It is. Which is why we need to get it right. But "right" doesn't always mean "most complex." Sometimes it means "ships fastest and works for the user."

Rohan: I respect that. But I've seen systems where architectural debt bites you in the third year. I don't want to be there.

Kavita: Fair. But I also don't want to spend ten weeks on a feature when we could ship in five and iterate. What if we compromise?

Rohan: What kind of compromise?

Kavita: We do OT for v1. You design it so that swapping in a CRDT later is possible — architecture it for flexibility. Then in v2, if we need it, we upgrade to CRDT.

Rohan: [pause] I could design the sync layer so it's abstracted. OT underneath, but the API is generic.

Kavita: That's smart. So we get to market faster, we preserve optionality, and you don't feel like we're building garbage.

Rohan: I don't hate it.

Kavita: You don't have to love it. Just not hate it. Can you work with that?

Rohan: Yeah, I can do that. I'll design the abstraction.

Kavita: Good. And you'll still learn a lot about real-time systems and concurrency. That's the point anyway.

Rohan: True.

Kavita: Okay, who are you thinking for the implementation team?

Rohan: Mostly me for the sync engine. I'd want someone strong on the storage side. And I'd need a junior or two for the client-side integration work.

Kavita: Priya and Ishaan?

Rohan: Priya would be good. I'm less sure about Ishaan. He's fast but I don't know if he has the depth for the hard parts.

Kavita: What hard parts do you foresee?

Rohan: The deterministic ordering of operations. Handling clock skew. Network partitions and how we reconcile state. That's not trivial.

Kavita: Those are all things Ishaan could learn. Is there a reason you wouldn't want him to?

Rohan: [pause] I guess I'm worried about quality. I might just end up redoing his work.

Kavita: Or you might end up mentoring him through it. What if that was part of the goal?

Rohan: I'm not really a mentor.

Kavita: You could be. You're brilliant technically. If you spent time with Ishaan on the hard parts, he'd level up significantly.

Rohan: I'd rather ship fast than babysit.

Kavita: Those aren't mutually exclusive. Teaching someone is how they get better. And then next time you don't have to babysit.

Rohan: [pause] I hear you.

Kavita: Let me ask differently. We have two SEs who could be Staff-ready in a year or so if they get the right stretch work. Ishaan's one of them. If you gave him the network partition problem to solve with your guidance, what would happen?

Rohan: He'd probably solve it.

Kavita: And learn something.

Rohan: Yeah.

Kavita: And then we have another Staff Engineer in two years instead of waiting for whoever to level up organically.

Rohan: I get it. But that's not really my job, right?

Kavita: Actually, it is. As a Staff Engineer, one of your key OKRs is to mentor people toward Staff. This is the quarter to make that real.

Rohan: [pause] You've got a Staff engineer mentoring OKR on my review?

Kavita: I do. Two people toward Staff promotion candidates by year-end. This is how that happens.

Rohan: I didn't realize it was that formal.

Kavita: It should be. You're not just an IC anymore. You're helping shape the next generation of leaders. So yes, Ishaan on the hard parts. You guiding the work.

Rohan: Okay. I can do that.

Kavita: Good. Who's the other one? Priya?

Rohan: Maybe. She's solid but quieter than Ishaan. Harder to know what she's capable of.

Kavita: Fair. We can figure that out as we go. For now, collab engine is your big project. OT architecture, Priya on storage, Ishaan on the hard problems with your mentoring. July ship. Sound good?

Rohan: Yeah. Sounds good.

Kavita: Alright. Let's touch base in three weeks. I want to see the architecture design and your plan for mentoring Ishaan.

Rohan: Will do.

Kavita: Good. This is going to be a great quarter for you.

Rohan: Thanks.
