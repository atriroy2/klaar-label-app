Kavita: April update. How's the delegation shift going?

Vikram: It's harder than I thought. Last week there was a P2 in the query engine and I had to force myself not to jump in.

Kavita: Tell me what happened.

Vikram: Priya was on it. She spent three hours looking at it and then pinged me asking if I could take a look. And I said no. I said, "What haven't you tried yet?" Even though I wanted to just solve it.

Kavita: How did she react?

Vikram: She was surprised. But she went back and tried a different approach. Got it in another hour and a half.

Kavita: Four and a half hours instead of you solving it in one.

Vikram: Yeah.

Kavita: But what happened to Priya?

Vikram: [pause] She learned it. She was telling me about it afterwards and she actually understood the whole thing. The locks, the transaction ordering... I think she got it.

Kavita: She got it. That's the point. What's the worst that could happen if Priya handles a P2 without you?

Vikram: She could miss something. Make a mistake.

Kavita: Has she?

Vikram: Not yet.

Kavita: And if she did?

Vikram: We'd catch it in code review. And I'd help her fix it.

Kavita: Right. Is that worse than you doing it in one hour?

Vikram: No. I mean, the customer sees it, but...

Kavita: Would it be a critical issue if she made a small mistake?

Vikram: Probably not. It would be caught quickly.

Kavita: Okay. So the worst case is: she learns, or she makes a small mistake and learns. Either way, the outcome is better than you staying as the bottleneck.

Vikram: Yeah, I get it intellectually. It's just hard to watch.

Kavita: I know. But you're doing it. Keep going. How's the presentation thing going? Are people presenting their own work?

Vikram: It's weird. Priya presented the webhook stuff at the all-hands. She was nervous but really good. She actually explained the design choices better than I would have.

Kavita: How did that feel?

Vikram: Pride, actually. It was cool to watch her own it.

Kavita: Hold onto that feeling. That's what good management feels like. What about Amit?

Vikram: Amit's presenting the circuit breaker refactor next sprint. He's been working on it end-to-end like we planned. His estimates are getting better too. The Friday retros are still happening and he's owning them now, not me just running them.

Kavita: What changed?

Vikram: I stopped running them. I said, "Amit, you own this retro. Look at what you estimated versus what actually happened and tell me what you see." Now he comes prepared with three things he learned.

Kavita: Excellent. And Ravi?

Vikram: Ravi's still escalating regularly. The daily check-in is solid. He's doing more complex things now because he knows he can ask. He's even starting to mentor some of the interns on our Kafka setup.

Kavita: He's mentoring. That's growth. So how many incidents have you owned in the last month?

Vikram: I'd say 20 to 25 percent of them. We had that big outage in week three and I coordinated that, but the diagnosis came from the team.

Kavita: That's exactly where you should be. You're unblocking, you're coordinating, but they're solving. That's the 70/30 split we talked about, more or less.

Vikram: It feels good, honestly. I'm less exhausted.

Kavita: That's the other benefit. You're not as burned out. But your output on the technical front — what's happening there?

Vikram: I'm doing less deep technical work, which is the point. I'm design reviewing more, but I'm not in the code as much.

Kavita: Are you okay with that? Is that sustainable for you?

Vikram: Yeah. Actually, I think this is the right role for me. When I was doing both — managing and being the hero engineer — I was doing both badly.

Kavita: Right. Okay, so Q2 goals. You're tracking toward 60% migration?

Vikram: We should hit 65, actually. The team's moving well now.

Kavita: Nice. And the latency target?

Vikram: 220ms is looking realistic. The async query rewrite is paying off.

Kavita: And the 70/30 incident split?

Vikram: We're at 75/25 right now. So we're there.

Kavita: Fantastic. You've made a real shift in just four weeks. I want to see this continue. Keep the daily check-ins, keep the Friday estimation retro with Amit, keep letting your team present their work. Those are your three things.

Vikram: Got it.

Kavita: And when the urge comes to jump into an incident, what's your process?

Vikram: Breathe. Ask the person what they've tried. Set a time limit. And only join if they hit it and ask.

Kavita: Good. You've learned something important here. You're starting to become a real manager. Don't lose that.

Vikram: I won't. It's making a difference already.

Kavita: Alright, let's touch base in three weeks. Keep shipping.

Vikram: Will do.

Kavita: One question before we wrap. How are you feeling about your role as a manager? Is this what you want to be doing?

Vikram: [pause] Yeah, actually. I was worried that I'd miss being in the code. But developing people is surprisingly satisfying.

Kavita: That's good to hear. You're good at it. And it matters more at this stage of the company.

Vikram: I'm starting to see that. The migration wouldn't have shipped if I'd tried to do all the technical work myself.

Kavita: Exactly. You're becoming a multiplier. That's the journey.

Vikram: I like that framing.

Kavita: One more thing. Make sure you're celebrating the wins with the team. They shipped something massive. Don't move on too quickly to the next project.

Vikram: Good reminder. I'll make sure to do that.

Kavita: Alright, let's wrap. Good work this month.

Vikram: Thanks. Same time in three weeks?

Kavita: Yep. Enjoy the lighter workload next week.

Vikram: Will do.
